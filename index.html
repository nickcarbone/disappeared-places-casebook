The Case File of a Disappeared Place

Six places that were taken by eminent domain, bulldozed by a city, annihilated as reprisal, cleared under apartheid, demolished by treaty, or swallowed by a story. Each file reconstructs the place from maps, land and tax records, censuses, photographs and what survives on the ground, then separates what the record proves from what has been told about it.

DocumentedSupported by primary records (deeds, censuses, proclamations, court filings, contemporaneous photographs or film) and corroborated by an institutional or scholarly source.
Gradual declineA slow structural process, such as withheld services, neglect or earlier partial removals. Often the condition later cited to justify erasure.
LoreA widely circulated claim that the record contradicts or does not support. Includes period propaganda and later myth.
DisputedSources conflict on a figure or date, the record supports more than one reading, or a claim rests only on secondary retellings.
Method and limits

How this casebook was built

Every factual statement carries a bracketed number linking to that case’s source list. Sources were consulted online on or before 25 October 2026; no physical archive was visited. Where a figure or event appears only in popular or secondary retellings, the source is flagged secondary and the claim is classified Disputed unless corroborated.

The “then” and “now” maps are original schematics drawn for this page. They show relative positions of features described in surveys, archival maps, municipal records and site descriptions. They are not traced from any map, they are not to scale, and building counts are illustrative unless a figure is stated. Each map states its basis and a confidence level.

No historical photographs are reproduced. Instead, evidence cards describe specific photographs and films, name the institution that holds them and give catalogue references where published, so a reader can locate the originals.

Cases were selected because recovering the place changes how a consequential decision reads: who held power over land, how a label preceded a clearance, or how a myth displaced a documented crime. Six cases cannot support statistical claims; the cross-case patterns are offered as observations to test against other places.

Corrections: if a source has been superseded or a figure is wrong, the case’s source list shows where each claim came from, so it can be checked and amended.
